Hokkaido ski winter 2026-27 · written 11 November 2026

Why this winter's snow will come late, and when to go

Hokkaido gets its famous powder from a simple natural machine. This winter, several large forces push on that machine at once. Here is each one, in pictures.

The short answer Go between 16 January and 3 February. The best fortnight inside that is 19 January to 1 February. December is the risky month this year. February is a good second choice if you miss the week of 4 to 11 February. Furano is a sensible hedge: thinner snow, but less affected by El Niño.

First, how Hokkaido makes snow

Hokkaido does not wait for storms. It makes snow almost daily, the same way a cold wind makes steam rise off a hot bath.

Where the snow lands

The wind reaches Hokkaido's west side first, so the resorts there get the most snow. The centre, around Furano, gets about half as much. It sits further from the sea and is about 3 °C colder, so its snow is drier and less exposed to rain.

People often say the centre is less deep but more consistent. The official records for the two valley towns, 35 winters each, support the first half and only partly the second.

Less deepTrueAbout half the snowfall and under half the base, in every month.
More consistentPartlyNew snow is a little steadier. The base is not. Furano's worst December had 14 cm on the ground.
In strong El Niño wintersFurano held up betterJanuary snowfall was above usual in all six since 1982, and December lost less than at Niseko.

That last point matters this year. A poor December at Niseko also tells you little about Furano: the two Decembers barely move together. So Furano is a real hedge for an El Niño winter, as long as you accept a thinner base and less off-piste depth. Six winters is a small sample.

Force 1: a record El Niño weakens the wind

El Niño is a patch of unusually warm water in the tropical Pacific, far from Japan. It is large enough to bend the weather across half the planet. Near Japan it weakens the north-west wind that drives the snow machine, so cold spells arrive in shorter bursts with mild breaks between them.

So what did past strong El Niño winters do to the snow at Niseko? I checked 65 winters of official records from Kutchan, the weather station in the valley below the resort.

Force 2: a very warm sea cuts both ways

The Sea of Japan is much warmer than normal this autumn. A warm sea is fuel. When cold air does arrive, it picks up more moisture and can drop more snow in one storm. But warm water also warms the air just above it. Early in the season that can turn snow into rain at the bottom of the mountain.

Force 3: the Arctic's fence of wind has broken early

High above the North Pole, a ring of fast wind normally holds the coldest air in place, like a fence. Forecasters call it the polar vortex. When the ring is tight, the cold stays in the Arctic. When it wobbles, lobes of cold air spill south. This November the ring has almost stopped, weeks earlier than in any year in the record.

The part that never changes: December starts from zero

Skiing needs a base: enough packed snow to cover rocks, bamboo grass and stream beds, and to open the off-piste gates. December and January get the same amount of new snow in a normal year. The difference is what it lands on.

Picking the week

Months hide a lot. Kutchan has a reading for every day, so here are the odds for each week of the season, from 35 winters of daily records.

PowderOne day in six until late JanuaryFrom mid December to the end of January. In February it drops to one day in ten.
ThawsAlmost none from 5 January to 8 FebruaryOne day in three is a thaw day in the first week of December.
SunshineThe price of powderOne day in five is sunny in December, one in four in late January, one in two at the end of February.

In strong El Niño winters the base ran about two weeks late and stayed 20 to 30 cm lower all season. It reached one metre in the town around 10 January instead of the turn of the year. December also had about twice as many thaw days. But the two weeks from 19 January were the snowiest of those winters, with 80 to 90 cm of new snow a week against the usual 65. That is the best fortnight to aim for.

Furano has more sun in every week: about four days in ten are sunny from mid January, against one in four at Niseko (Furano daily records). Wind shows no pattern by week in the town records, and they cannot tell us about lift closures on the mountain.

Why Hokkaido and not Honshu this year

Hakuba, Nozawa Onsen and Myoko on the main island get their snow from the same machine. They sit 700 km further south, where the air is closer to freezing, so a weaker wind and a warmer winter cost them much more.

What the official forecast says

Japan's weather agency gives odds, not a single prediction. For snowfall on Hokkaido's Sea of Japan side this winter it leans slightly toward less.

How sure is this?

HighJanuary is the safest month. This is true every year, El Niño or not. MediumDecember will be below normal. History points that way, but it rests on a handful of winters, and the leaky Arctic could cancel it. LowHow the whole season adds up. This El Niño is stronger than any before it, so the past is a rough guide.

If the trip has to be in December, book late with free cancellation. Two things to watch: the agency's next three-month outlook on 24 November, and whether Kutchan has about 60 cm on the ground by 10 December. In a typical year it has about 45 cm by then.
