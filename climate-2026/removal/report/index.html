Written 2026-11-12 by Claude, with two research workers. This is a second part of the climate analysis. That report is about how to stop emissions. This one asks a different question: can we take CO2 out of the air again, with trees or with technology, and how much does that help?

Answer

  1. Removal is necessary, but it is the small part of the solution. Paths that keep warming well below 2 °C cut emissions by about 80 to 90% and remove the rest. They need about 7 to 9 Gt of CO2 removal per year by 2050 S. The world emits about 43 Gt of CO2 per year D. Removal cannot replace the cuts.
  2. Almost all removal today is trees. People remove about 2.2 Gt per year, and more than 99.9% of that is forest S. All the technical methods together remove about 2 million tonnes per year C. That is 0.005% of emissions.
  3. Each 0.1 °C costs about 180 to 220 Gt. That is four to five years of the present CO2 emissions C. To lower the temperature by 0.1 °C in 50 years, the world must remove about 4 Gt per year more than it emits C. So removal can shave a peak. It cannot bring back the old climate.
  4. Trees are cheap and ready, but they need much land and they are not permanent. One Gt per year needs about 90 million hectares of new tropical forest, or about 200 million hectares in the temperate zone C. A realistic share for new forest is about 1 to 4 Gt per year S. Fire, drought and logging can release the carbon again. To protect the forests that exist gives more than to plant new ones.
  5. Machines are permanent, but they are tiny, expensive and they need much energy. Direct air capture costs about $670 per tonne now S. One Gt per year needs about 2,000 TWh, which is 6% of the world's electricity C. The largest plant in operation removed 675 tonnes in half a year S. That is half a second of world emissions C.
  6. The technical methods must grow about 450 times by 2050. That is 29% per year for 24 years C. Solar power grew 27% per year in the last ten years D. It is possible, but solar sells a product. Removal sells a waste service that nobody must buy.
  7. The largest risk is not the technology. It is the excuse. A promise of future removal makes it easier to delay cuts today. A tonne that is not emitted costs less than $100 for most sources. A tonne removed by machine costs $200 to $700. The correct order is: cut emissions, protect the forests and the sinks, then use removal for the last 10 to 20%.

1. Why removal, when we can stop emissions?

There are three reasons.

  • Some emissions are very hard to stop. Examples are methane and nitrous oxide from farms, CO2 from the chemistry of cement, and long flights. National plans expect that about 18% of today's emissions remain in 2050 S Buck et al. 2023. "Net zero" means that removal balances this rest.
  • The CO2 stays. When emissions stop, warming stops, but the temperature does not go down again for centuries M. Only removal lowers it.
  • The world will pass 1.5 °C. UNEP says that an overshoot is now almost certain, and that removal must take out about five years of current CO2 emissions for each 0.1 °C that the world wants to come back S UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2025.

My own numbers agree with UNEP. The carbon budget table in the data gives 183 Gt of CO2 for each 0.1 °C between 1.5 and 1.7 °C D. The IPCC relation between CO2 and temperature (0.45 °C per 1,000 Gt) gives 222 Gt, with a range of 159 to 370 Gt C. The world emits 43.4 Gt of CO2 per year (fossil 39.0, land use 4.35) D. So 0.1 °C is 4.2 to 5.1 years of emissions C.

This also works in the other direction. Each year of emissions at the present rate adds about 0.02 °C C, and it needs 43 Gt of later removal to undo.

2. What nature does without us

The ocean and the land already take up about half of the CO2 that people emit.

Sink, 2024 Gt CO2 per year Share of emissions, 2015-2024
Ocean 12.5 29%
Land (plants and soils) 7.0 21%

Source: Global Carbon Budget 2025 S; the conversion from carbon to CO2 is C.

Three facts about these sinks are important.

  • They are not "removal". They happen without our action, because there is more CO2 in the air. The carbon budgets and the temperature numbers already include them. If a country counts this natural uptake against its fossil emissions, the sum says "net zero" but the warming continues S Allen et al. 2024. National inventories already do this: they report 6.7 Gt per year more land uptake than the science models S Grassi et al. 2023.
  • The land sink becomes weaker. Climate change has made the land sink 25% smaller and the ocean sink 7% smaller than they would be S Global Carbon Project. In the hot year 2023, the land took up about 1.6 Gt, the least since 2003 S Ke et al. 2024. The fires in Canada in 2023 emitted about 2.4 Gt S Byrne et al. 2024. The south-east Amazon is now a source S. The forests of Finland have been a source since 2021 S Statistics Finland.
  • To keep them is the first task. A sink of 19 Gt per year is nine times all deliberate removal C. A loss of 10% of it cancels all the tree planting in the world.

Sources: State of Carbon Dioxide Removal, 3rd edition, June 2026 and Carbon Brief.

Total removal must become four times larger C. The technical methods must become about 450 times larger, which is 29% growth per year until 2050 C. For comparison, solar electricity grew 26.9% per year from 2015 to 2025 D. The report itself says that removal "might need to scale faster than solar" S.

In the IPCC scenarios, the removal in 2050 comes from three places: forests and soils 3.0 Gt, bioenergy with capture 2.75 Gt, and direct air capture 0.02 Gt (medians) S IPCC AR6 WG3, chapter 12. The models choose bioenergy because it was the cheapest method in their cost tables, not because it is easy.

Even the full 8.75 Gt per year does not cool the planet much. If the world removed that much for 50 years, and emitted nothing, the temperature would fall by about 0.2 °C C. In the scenarios, most of that removal only balances the emissions that remain.

4. Trees

How much they can do

The estimates fall when the method becomes more careful.

Study Result Note
Bastin et al. 2019 205 Gt of carbon (750 Gt CO2) on 0.9 billion ha Disputed. Critics found it about five times too large; the authors published a correction S
Mo et al. 2023 226 Gt of carbon (828 Gt CO2), range 151-363 61% is in forests that exist, if we let them grow old. New forest is 87 Gt of carbon S
Cook-Patton et al. 2020 8.9 Gt CO2 per year to 2050 on 678 million ha The technical maximum for natural regrowth S
Griscom et al. 2017 11.3 Gt CO2e per year below $100 per tonne All "natural climate solutions"; much of it is avoided emissions, not removal S
IPCC AR6 WG3, chapter 7 3.9 Gt per year technical (0.5-10.1); 1.2 to 1.6 Gt below $100 New forest only S
Busch et al. 2024 31 Gt in 30 years below $50 per tonne About 1 Gt per year, in poor and middle-income countries S

A fair summary: new forest can remove about 1 to 4 Gt per year for some decades, at $10 to $50 per tonne. That is real and useful. It is 2 to 9% of present CO2 emissions.

The upper limit is also useful. If the complete Mo et al. potential were reached, it would be 828 Gt, or 19 years of present emissions, and it would lower the temperature by at most 0.37 °C C. The new-forest part is 0.14 °C C. This takes 50 to 100 years, and it happens only once: a grown forest takes up no more.

Land and time

  • A young tropical forest takes up about 11 tonnes of CO2 per hectare per year in the first 20 years, and needs a median of 66 years to reach 90% of the biomass of an old forest S Poorter et al. 2016.
  • So 1 Gt per year needs about 90 million hectares in the wet tropics C. With a temperate rate of 5 tonnes it is 200 million hectares, and with a boreal rate of 2 tonnes it is 500 million C, but these two rates are from memory M.
  • The 3.0 Gt per year in the IPCC median needs about 265 million hectares of tropical land C. India is 329 million hectares M. The Cook-Patton maximum needs 678 million hectares, which is two times India or 45% of the world's cropland C.

What goes wrong

  • The carbon can come back. California keeps a reserve of offset credits against fire for 100 years. Fires used at least 95% of it in less than ten years S Badgley et al. 2022. Fossil CO2 stays in the air for thousands of years. A forest must stand that long to balance it.
  • Planted trees die. At 176 sites in tropical Asia, 44% of the young trees were dead after five years S Banin et al.. A study of 430 plantations in north India found no increase in tree cover after decades S Coleman et al. 2021.
  • Plantations are not forests. About 45% of the national pledges are commercial plantations, which are cut every ten years or so. Natural forest stores about 40 times more carbon in the long term S Lewis et al. 2019.
  • Dark trees warm. Forest is darker than snow, grass or desert and absorbs more sunlight. A count of carbon alone can be up to 81% too high. The effect is largest in the north and in dry land S Hasler et al. 2024. So the tropics are the correct place for new forest.
  • Land is in use. New forest competes with food, and trees on natural grassland damage it.

Protect first

A hectare of old tropical forest that is cut releases in a few years what a new hectare needs about 66 years to take up again. Regrowth in the humid tropics balances only 26% of the emissions from forest loss there S Heinrich et al. 2023. The world still loses 4.3 million hectares of primary tropical forest per year (main report, section 8). To stop that loss is faster, cheaper and more certain than to plant.

6. Machines: direct air capture

A direct air capture (DAC) plant moves air across a chemical that binds CO2. Heat then releases the CO2, and a pump puts it deep into rock.

The physics

CO2 is 0.04% of air. To separate it needs energy, and physics sets a minimum of about 0.12 MWh per tonne S. Real plants need about 1.5 to 2.5 MWh per tonne, which is 12 to 20 times the minimum C Keith et al. 2018. There is room to improve, but not to zero.

Plants of the size of Stratos (0.5 Mt per year) 2,000 C

If machines did all of the 8.75 Gt, they would need 17,500 TWh, which is 55% of the electricity that the world makes today C.

Clean power is better used on the grid first. Two MWh of clean electricity remove 1 tonne with DAC. The same two MWh avoid 1.9 tonnes if they replace coal power [C, with a coal value from memory], and 0.9 tonnes if they replace average grid power (458 g per kWh D). DAC makes sense only where the grid is already clean or where the power cannot be used for something else. Iceland is such a place.

The state today

  • Mammoth (Climeworks, Iceland, 2024) is the largest plant in operation. Its design is 36,000 tonnes per year. Its net removal was 119 tonnes in the first half of 2025 and 675 tonnes in the first half of 2026 S Net Zero Compare. That is 4% of the design C. The world emits 1,374 tonnes of CO2 per second C.
  • Stratos (Occidental, Texas) has a design of 500,000 tonnes per year. Its start moved from 2025 to the end of 2026. I found no report that it has captured CO2 S E&E News. Its owner is an oil company, and US law now pays the same $180 per tonne when the CO2 is used to push more oil out of old fields S Global CCS Institute. That use is not removal.
  • All plants in the world capture about 0.01 Mt per year. The planned projects give about 3 Mt in 2030, and the IEA net zero path needs about 75 Mt S IEA. This number is from about 2023.

The cost

$ per tonne Label
Supplier break-even, 2025 670 S CDR.fyi and OPIS
Climeworks target for 2030 400 to 600 S
Independent estimate at 1 Gt per year 340 (226 to 835) S Sievert et al. 2024
US government target below 100 S DOE
Most emission cuts below 100, half of them below 20 S main report, section 9

Will DAC follow the cost curve of solar? I think only partly. Solar panels are small, identical and made in factories by the billion. A DAC plant is partly like that (the capture boxes) and partly a chemical plant with large fans, heat and pipes, and chemical plants become cheaper slowly. The energy cost also has a floor. The independent estimate of about $340 at very large scale looks more credible to me than $100.

7. Other technical methods, and the storage

Method How it works Potential, Gt per year Cost per tonne now State
Bioenergy with capture (BECCS) Burn or ferment plants, capture the CO2, store it in rock 0.5 to 5 with limits for land about 230 Two US ethanol plants have a capacity of about 1.8 Mt per year. Stockholm builds 0.8 Mt per year for 2028. Drax in the UK left its project in 2026 S
Enhanced rock weathering Spread crushed basalt on fields; it binds CO2 as it dissolves 0.5 to 2 270 to 350 Thousands of tonnes verified. Hard to measure in the field; some trials found nothing S
Ocean alkalinity Add alkaline minerals to seawater, which then takes up more CO2 not known; large in theory about 270 First 626 verified tonnes in 2025. A trial in Cornwall was stopped after protests S
Storage in rock Pump CO2 into deep saline rock or basalt prudent total 1,460 Gt 10 to 30 M Works. In Iceland more than 95% became stone in two years S

BECCS has the same land problem as trees, and a larger one. The 1.5 °C scenarios used 93 to 724 million hectares of energy crops S Chatham House. It makes most sense with waste: ethanol plants, paper mills, and the burning of household waste.

Storage is not the limit in this century, but it is not without limit. A study in 2025 put the prudent world capacity at 1,460 Gt, after it excluded places with earthquake risk, groundwater or many people S Gidden et al. 2025. If all of it were used for removal, it would lower the temperature by about 0.7 °C C.

Capture at a chimney is not removal. A filter on a gas power plant or a cement kiln stops new fossil CO2. That is a cut in emissions. Only CO2 that came from the air (through a plant or a machine) and stays out of the air counts as removal S State of CDR, chapter 1.

Sources for the table: CDR.fyi and OPIS prices, Stockholm Exergi, Drax, Beerling et al. 2020, weathering trials, Planetary credits, Cornwall, Carbfix.

The "realistic in 2050" row is my judgement from the ranges in the studies above, not a result from one source. The sum is about 3 to 12 Gt per year. The need is 7 to 9. So the target can be reached only if several methods work, and none of them is certain.

No method is cheap, permanent and large at the same time. Trees are cheap and large but not permanent. Machines are permanent but not cheap and not yet large.

9. Who pays?

A removed tonne has no buyer by nature. It is a waste service, like sewage treatment. Somebody must be made to pay, or the state must pay.

  • Buyers have ordered about 49 Mt of durable removal in total, and 1.67 Mt has been delivered, which is 3.4% C from CDR.fyi S. All durable removal that was ever delivered equals 20 minutes of world CO2 emissions C.
  • One company, Microsoft, made about 90% of the purchases in 2025. In April 2026 it was reported to have paused new purchases; the company denies that the programme has ended S Heatmap. A market with one buyer is fragile.
  • Public demand starts slowly. The EU certified its first permanent removal methods in 2026 and proposes to let removals into its carbon market after 2030. Sweden and Denmark buy bioenergy capture by auction. The US kept its tax credit of $180 per tonne S.

The cost at full scale C:

World GDP is about $117 trillion S. The world spends about 2.5% of GDP on its armies M, so the middle case is of that size. It is possible to pay. But the last row shows the main point: each 0.1 °C that we let pass now costs tens of trillions of dollars to take back later. To avoid the same emissions costs less than a third of that.

10. The risks of the idea itself

  • Delay. If governments and companies count on cheap removal in 2050, they cut less now. The removal then does not arrive, or it arrives late and small. Researchers call this "mitigation deterrence" and propose separate targets for cuts and for removal S McLaren et al. 2019. "Net-zero oil" from Occidental is the clearest example.
  • A tonne is not a tonne. A tonne in a tree for 40 years does not balance a tonne of fossil CO2 that stays for thousands of years. The Oxford principles say: use removal only for the emissions that cannot be avoided, and move to permanent storage by the net zero date S Oxford.
  • Weak credits. Forest credits sell for about $22 per tonne and the low price reflects the doubt S. The main report cites a review in which less than 16% of offset credits were real reductions.
  • Removal cools a little less than emission warms M. The ocean gives back part of the CO2 that it took up when the air had more of it. So my numbers per 0.1 °C are a best case.

11. My judgement

  1. Removal is the last 10 to 20% of the solution, and it is not a reason to wait. The cheap tonnes are the ones that are never emitted.
  2. The best "removal" action now is to keep the forests and the natural sinks. They do 19 Gt per year without payment, and they become weaker as the planet warms.
  3. Plant and regrow forest where it fits: in the wet tropics, with native trees, with the people who live there, and counted as a bonus for some decades. Do not sell it as a licence to burn fossil fuel.
  4. Build the technical methods now, with public money, because they need 20 years to grow. The target is not tonnes today. The target is to learn what works and what it costs: 30% growth per year, as solar had. Governments should buy removal as they buy waste treatment, by auction, with strict measurement.
  5. Keep two separate accounts, one for emission cuts and one for removal, and inside removal one for permanent and one for temporary storage.
  6. For one person: to pay for removal is a gift to a young industry, not a way to cancel a flight. At $200 to $700 per durable tonne, the same money cuts far more through the groups in section 11 of the main report. Forest credits at $10 to $25 are useful for nature but they do not balance fossil CO2.

What could not be verified

  • No page was opened. Every fact from a paper, a report or a news site is S, and the numbers for 2026 cannot be checked from memory.
  • The IPCC numbers come from summaries and one draft chapter text, not from the final chapters.
  • The 8.75 Gt need in 2050 is from secondary reports on the State of CDR 2026. An errata file exists for that edition and was not read.
  • The removal by technical methods (about 2 Mt per year) is 0.1% of 2.2 Gt. The 2024 edition gave 1.3 Mt.
  • Temperate and boreal growth rates, the coal emission factor, the storage cost, the area of India and of cropland, and military spending are from memory.
  • Mammoth numbers differ between sources (gross or net). Stratos may have started after the search.
  • The "realistic in 2050" row in section 8 is my judgement.
  • The cost table uses one price for all tonnes. Real costs differ by method and fall with time.
