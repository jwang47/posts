Climate change in 2026, part two

Carbon removal: what trees and machines can do

Stopping emissions stops the warming. Only taking CO2 back out of the air can lower the temperature again. It is necessary, it works, and it is the small part of the answer.

One year of CO2, one dot per million tonnes

43,350 dots: what people emit in a year. Oceans, plants and soils absorb the grey part without being asked. The orange part stays in the air.

2,200 dots: what people remove on purpose, almost all with forests.

2 dots: what all technology removes, from charcoal to machines. They are inside the circle.

Paths that keep warming below 2 °C need about four of those blue strips by 2050: 7 to 9 billion tonnes (Gt) a year. They need the orange and grey field to shrink by four fifths first.

Removal is the last step, and delay makes it larger

Some emissions are very hard to stop: methane from farms, the chemistry of cement, long flights. "Net zero" is the year when removal balances that rest. Until then, everything between the two lines is added to the air, and it stays there. Move the slider to start the cuts later.

Each 0.1 °C is about 183 to 222 Gt of CO2, which is 4.2 to 5.1 years of present emissions. Removal can shave a peak. It cannot bring back the old climate.

Trees: cheap, ready, and they need a continent

New forest can realistically remove 1 to 4 Gt a year for some decades, at $10 to $50 a tonne. Nothing else is close to that today. The first limit is land.

The land that new tropical forest needs

1 Gt a year: 0.9 million km² of young tropical forest (small square) All 8.75 Gt, if forest did it alone: 7.8 million km² (large square) For scale: India is 3.2 and Australia 7.7 million km²

Equal-area map, so the squares can be compared with any country. Outside the tropics trees grow slower and the squares would be more than twice as large.

  • It happens once. A tropical forest needs about 66 years to regain 90% of its mass, and then it stops absorbing.
  • It can come back out. California set aside forest credits to cover a hundred years of fires. Fires used at least 95% of them in under ten years.
  • Protecting beats planting. A cut hectare of old forest releases in a few years what a new hectare needs most of a lifetime to take back, and the world still loses 4.3 million hectares a year.

Machines: permanent, tiny, and hungry

A direct air capture plant filters CO2 from air and pumps it deep into rock, where it stays. The largest one running, Mammoth in Iceland, was designed for 36,000 tonnes a year and removed 675 tonnes in the first half of 2026.

CO2 is only 0.04% of air, so capture takes about 2 megawatt-hours per tonne. One Gt a year would take 6.3% of the world's electricity. While grids still burn coal, that clean power does more good elsewhere:

The technical methods must grow about 450 times by 2050, or 29% a year. Solar grew 27% a year over the last decade, so the pace is possible. But solar sells electricity. Removal sells a waste service that nobody has to buy: one company made about 90% of the purchases in 2025.

For comparison: not emitting the tonne

Most emission cuts cost under $100 a tonne, and half of them under $20. The mark on each cost bar is $100.

At $340 a tonne, taking back a single tenth of a degree costs $62 to 76 trillion.

So, in order

  1. Cut emissions first. Removal is for the last 10 to 20%, not a reason to wait. The promise of removal later is the easiest excuse for cutting less now.
  2. Keep the forests and the natural sinks. The grey dots are free, and they are weakening as the planet warms.
  3. Regrow forest where it fits: in the wet tropics, with native trees and the people who live there, counted as a bonus and not as a licence to burn.
  4. Build the technical methods now with public money. They need twenty years to grow. Governments should buy removal the way they buy waste treatment.
  5. Keep two accounts, one for cuts and one for removal, so that one cannot hide the lack of the other.
